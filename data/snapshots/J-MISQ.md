Some years ago, while working on _MISQ_’s special issue on Next-Generation Theory, a close colleague asked whether _MISQ_ could really produce “next generation” theory because the journal’s stature as a “top journal” might force it to be conservative. I remember being surprised because I did not view _MISQ_ as conservative at all. I felt _MISQ_ was inherently innovative.

As I enter the last four months of my term as editor-in-chief, I have been reflecting on this question. I’d like to cover three topics in this editorial that I hope will be helpful for readers. I wish to:

- -
convey how innovation is part of _MISQ_’s DNA, especially for anyone who may have thought of it as conservative

- -
outline how the editorial board and I have continued _MISQ_’s spirit of innovation during this editorial term

- -
describe some innovations that _MISQ_ will need to consider in the future, especially regarding generative AI


I adapted the title of this editorial from Weick’s (1985) well-known paper. His description of editing innovation in _Administrative Science Quarterly_ ( _ASQ_) focused on how a journal can improve the innovativeness of its output through inviting papers, crafting special issues, and providing high-quality reviews and copyediting. While this editorial has the same spirit, I take a broader view of innovation. Like Weick, I will convey how _MISQ_ is the kind of journal that strives to publish the most innovative work possible. More broadly, _MISQ_ is also the kind of journal that is willing to reflect on what an academic journal is, could be, and should be, and willing to reinvent itself when needed while still celebrating core values. _MISQ_ is not a journal that simply stands still, resting on its laurels.

I’ll start with a brief review of _MISQ_’s innovative history and introduce a framework I use to think about innovation in journals. I’ll then describe the innovations that the editorial board and I have worked on and the process we followed during my term as EIC. I’ll conclude with the thoughts of some past and current editors to give a sense of the “long view” of innovation at _MISQ_.

## A Journal Born of Innovation

The very first words printed in _MISQ_ were: “With this issue, the _MIS Quarterly_ attempts to break new ground in the information systems field” (Dickson, 1977). As these words imply, _MISQ_ was a product of innovation from the outset.

I find it impossible to imagine what it was like when _MISQ_ was founded because it requires thinking about our field _before_ it was a field. Like so many other scholars in our field, I typically take our institutions for granted. We have strong departments of information systems around the world, a large portfolio of journals and conferences, a vibrant professional association, celebrities young and old, and well-told stories and myths. Nevertheless, while our field has institutionalized, I do not believe our field’s spirit for innovation has ever waned. A deep respect for innovation is part of our field’s soul. After all, that is what encourages so many of us to enter a field such as ours—oriented around an ever-evolving phenomenon—rather than getting into a field oriented around something stable. The institutions of our field, such as _MISQ_, surely need to be innovative because our field is _all about_ innovation.

Everyone associated with _MISQ_ can and, to some extent, _must_ engage in innovation. Authors innovate when they conduct and submit novel work. Reviewers and editors innovate when they decide to review or edit in a new way. The _MISQ_ publication office innovates when it creates new roles and hires staff with new ideas and approaches. The journal needs innovations from all its stakeholders.

Even if we concentrate mainly on the innovations shaped by the journal’s editors-in-chief, the history of _MISQ_ is full of innovation. To give a sense of the variety of innovations we have seen, consider the following small sample:

- -
_New sections_: The “Research Essay” section (later called “Methods Articles”) started in 1996 and the “Review” section (later called “Theory and Review”) started in 2001 (Zmud, 1996; Watson, 2001).

- -
_New mission_: The mission statement was altered in 2006 to explicitly stress topics beyond managerial issues (Saunders, 2006).

- -
_New alliance: MISQ_ and the Association for Information Systems forged a formal alliance in May 2002 (Weber, 2002).

- -
_New reviewing system_: _MISQ_ made over 100 configurations when adopting the ScholarOne reviewing system to establish its way of reviewing and editing papers (Straub, 2010).

- -
_New models of peer review_: _MISQ_ tested non-blind reviews in 20001 and wiki-based reviews in 2009 (Kane & Fichman, 2009).

- -
_New disclosures_: In 2005, _MISQ_ began temporarily disclosing reviewer names on published articles (Saunders, 2005).

- -
_New services_: In 2016, _MISQ_ started its Curations initiative (Rai, 2016), see [https://misq.umn.edu/research-curations](https://misq.umn.edu/research-curations).

- -
_Discontinued practices_: _MISQ_ ceased the reject-resubmit option in 2013 and ceased publishing online supplements in 2020.


Once we start laying out these innovations (and others that will be described later), it becomes clear that the landscape of potential innovations for academic journals is very broad. We see product innovation, process innovation, platform innovation, and even institutional innovation. How can we conceive of the types of innovation and the best way to engage in them? Clearly, there is no “one right way” to conceive of these issues because an academic journal, just like science in general, is an evolving social construction (Nowotny et al., 2001; Linkov et al., 2006). Each editor must instead craft their own conception and collaborate with others on a collective vision.

## MISQ Innovations 2021-2023: A Framework and a Set of Initiatives

In Figure 1, I show the framework that I used to think about innovations in academic journals during my term as EIC. I am aware of many concepts that can be used to think about innovation, such as processes of change (Poole et al., 2000), pressures against change (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Battilana & D’Aunno, 2009), and alternative forms of innovation (Henderson & Clark, 1990). However, I generally did not find such concepts helpful for thinking about the landscape for innovation at _MISQ_ because they are not specific to journals. In the end, I constructed the framework below, drawing from semiotics and hermeneutics (Ricoeur, 1973; Lee & Dennis, 2012; Chandler, 2007).

The basic idea in Figure 1 is that the scientific record—the great, interconnected network of papers spread throughout journals and digital platforms—can be thought of as its own world, the world of the text. This world of the text talks about a world in front of it (the real world we study and hope to inform) and it is a product of the world behind it (the world of researchers and their norms and practices).

When thinking about the innovations we pursue, we can think of any of these three worlds and their interrelationships. For instance, we can innovate to address changes in the world _of_ the text (e.g., changes in online journal platforms), changes in the world behind the text (e.g., changes in scientific norms or incentives), and changes in front of the text (e.g., real-world trends that necessitate new research). To understand the dynamics facing science and journals at any time, we can ask the sort of questions shown along the arrows in the figure.

During my EIC term, this framework has helped me reflect on the trends, challenges, and opportunities in all three worlds (behind, in front, and of the text). It has also helped me learn the constraints a journal faces. For instance, as mentioned in our last editorial (Burton-Jones & Wang, 2023), the dynamics in the world _of_ the text are now very influential, and I expect this influence will only increase. Many innovations can be considered in this world, such as the use of complementary online research repositories, the increased reach and sophistication of search and recommendation platforms, the semantic markup of scientific text, innovations in publication workflows and curations, the use of social media for promotion, and the creative packaging and pricing of articles. A contemporary academic journal must consider all these innovations and more, but editorial boards and publication offices have not traditionally had to consider them. As a small university publisher, the _MISQ_ publication office is also at a disadvantage compared to large, for-profit publishing houses (or even large association publishers) in funding major initiatives in these areas. As readers of the journal, if you have felt that _MISQ_ is slower than some other journals in innovating in the world of text, I humbly ask you to be patient. The _MISQ_ editorial board and publication office are working on them. We have constraints but we also have very important benefits (e.g., greater independence) compared to other journals. Our goal is to maximize the benefits and minimize the constraints.

By reflecting on the landscape of innovations that we face (Figure 1) and mapping that landscape to our editorial board’s scope of control and stakeholder vision, I worked with the board to devise a portfolio of innovations for this editorial term, shown in Table 1.

Collectively, the innovations in Table 1 reflect the portfolio of innovation that the _MISQ_ editorial board and I have worked on during my EIC term. Just like any organization that balances exploration and exploitation (March, 1991), we have worked on these innovations while sustaining core values (such as our big-tent philosophy) and performing our day-to-day work of processing papers, performing ambassadorial work, and continuing past innovations that have now become part-and-parcel of the journal (e.g., author development workshops, reviewing workshops, and the _MISQ_ curation).2

Clearly, the innovations in Table 1 vary in complexity. For instance, the knowledge-sharing sessions and masterclasses are simple and can be started/stopped at any time. Likewise, the open report and the impact award only require attention annually. In contrast, the transparency policy, DEI statement, and the test of innovations in the editorial process all required significant planning.

The innovations also vary in their degrees of permanence. For instance, the cross-disciplinary publication with _ASQ_ was a singular initiative. It offers a model for what the journal could do in the future, but it does not obligate the journal to a long commitment. The Scholarly Development Academy was similarly motivated by a singular event (COVID-19), and so this initiative could evolve as the pandemic subsides. The common characteristic of both initiatives is that they are significant but still temporary commitments. As a result, they can be continued (or not) in the same or different form over time. This gives each new EIC flexibility to choose the initiatives that will offer the most value at any given time. In contrast to such initiatives, the transparency policy and diversity, equity, and inclusion initiative are designed to be long-lived commitments. Such initiatives will often require additional decisions to be made over time, e.g., with the transparency policy, two key decisions that will need to be made over time include whether to recruit dedicated transparency editors to the board and how to implement tags on articles to help readers search for papers with data, code, or materials; Burton-Jones et al., 2021, xii).

In addition to knowing _what_ innovations the _MISQ_ board has worked on, it is helpful to know _how_ we innovate. There is nothing written down that tells an EIC or the board how to innovate. Nonetheless, you may be interested to know that the basic rule conveyed to me by the _MISQ_ Policy Committee3 was that simple initiatives that do not have implications beyond the current EIC’s term can be led by the EIC and started and stopped at will. Examples in Table 1 include the knowledge-sharing sessions, masterclasses, and invited submissions. In contrast, complex initiatives that have implications beyond the current EIC’s term require deliberation with the editorial board (and perhaps from the field in general) and require the _MISQ_ Policy Committee’s approval. For instance, if you read the editorials introducing _MISQ’s_ transparency policy and DEI statement, you will see the extensive consultation we engaged in to design and obtain support for those initiatives.

Readers should know that the editorial board does not innovate on a whim. Rather, the innovations in Table 1 were preceded by a detailed statement outlining _MISQ’s_ stakeholder vision, which in turn benefited from earlier consultation with the editorial board and policy committee (Burton-Jones, 2021). Moreover, other than the cross-disciplinary publication with _ASQ_, all the innovations in Table 1 were developed in consultation with multiple _MISQ_ board members. During 2021, the editorial board and I also went through a three-stage brainstorming process over four months during which we identified 13 innovations we could test, which we ultimately whittled down to the two deemed to have the most potential value (open reports and registered reports).

The editorial board is careful to draw on evidence for its innovations. For instance, even though the editorial board and I saw great merit in the registered report model of peer review, we did not feel that we had enough evidence regarding its usefulness for _MISQ_ at this stage. Therefore, we chose to test the approach first so that we could gain evidence and share it with our community before making any decisions about whether the journal should implement it as a permanent submission option.

Finally, as EIC, I should be willing to admit when I fail to identify or implement a worthy innovation. My personal example involves collaborating with practitioner-related academic journals. For instance, I personally believe that _MISQ_ and _MISQE_ should collaborate more deeply so that we can support each other in serving our joint external stakeholders (e.g., practitioners, policy makers). At first glance, I thought such a collaboration would be simple and valuable for both journals. In 2021, I benefited from brainstorming with Gabe Piccoli (then EIC of _MISQE_) but we were not able to find a clear win-win. Iris Junglas (current EIC of _MISQE_) and I did so again recently. It seems clear that we should take advantage of our joint branding and history to work together for our external stakeholders. Several ideas could be pursued, but deciding what is best may still take time.4

## A Longer-Term Perspective on Innovation at MISQ

Having described how we have innovated at _MISQ_ from 2021-2023, I’d like to reiterate my earlier point that innovation is part of _MISQ’s_ DNA by looking back at the journal’s spirit of innovation over time. I first asked Blake Ives for his views, as he was heavily involved with the journal over so many of the early years and is well-known for his own innovative spirit. His reflections are presented below:

## _MISQ_’s Spirit of Innovation – Blake Ives (Editor-in-Chief, 1992-1994)

As a doctoral student in 1977, I was assigned by _MISQ’s_ founding editor Gary Dickson, to proofread a paper intended for the very first issue. In subsequent years I published, reviewed, and was on the editorial board for the journal, but until I inherited the senior editorship in 1992, I never understood the challenges facing the journal. I found a journal caught between two masters: money and credibility. Much of the money came from the Society for Information Management (SIM), which had been funding the journal since its founding. The other master, academic credibility, was still to be fully earned from promotion and tenure committees.

As a member of the SIM publications committee, I found SIM leaders to be dissatisfied and questioning the relevance of our articles to their members. Meanwhile, the few papers that the journal still published that were intended for a practitioner audience were lightning rods for the senior faculty from other disciplines. The problem, extending back at least to Bill King’s appointment as the second head editor in 1983, could be traced to the innovation of Dickson, his predecessor, who had positioned the journal for “a vast majority of … practitioners” and for “a few people in academia associated with information systems who needed a place to publish their work” (Dickson, 1982, p. iv-v).

Dickson’s dichotomy was a Faustian bargain, leaving neither set of essential stakeholders happy. Bill King, well familiar with P&T deliberations, recognized the importance of establishing credibility with senior colleagues in other disciplines. During his term as editor-in-chief, he pushed the journal toward academic credibility and, as a consequence, away from practice.

Like any start-up, _MISQ_’s foremost objective was survival. Without money it would die. With far too small a pool of faculty to subscribe or to influence university librarians, SIM’s financial support was essential. Recognizing SIM’s unhappiness, the Minnesota-based publisher of the journal, Jim Wetherbe, convinced Harvard’s F. Warren McFarlan to take over as _MISQ_’s third editor-in-chief, in the hopes that it would stem SIM’s dissatisfaction, as McFarlan had an unmatched and enviable reputation with the SIM membership (Ives et al., in press).

McFarlan did several innovative things to make the journal more appealing to SIM members including writing overviews of each article in which he explained why the work might be important to practitioners. It bought the journal time, as did McFarlan’s insistence that he be appointed to the SIM executive committee. From there, he could reinforce to them the importance of safeguarding the field’s “seed corn” of young faculty who were essential to educating their future employees.

This chunk of ancient history illustrates the power of early _MISQ_ editors to almost instantaneously shape or reshape a journal. That is but one of many examples: Jim Emery, the fourth editor, pushed _MISQ_ to be more international; several of us opened it to more qualitative research—Allen Lee opened the journal to design science and convinced Alan Dennis, Jack Rockart, Jeanne Ross, and others to spin-off the practitioner-facing _MIS Quarterly Executive_. There are many more such examples.

Through my tenure as the fifth editor-in-chief, the only role of the _MISQ_ oversight board was to select the next editor. Once appointed, the editor was all-powerful. Mistakes were made, such as, perhaps, attempting too soon to move away from SIM, or my own stillborn attempt to establish a new digital outlet, _MISQ Discovery_ (Ives, 1996).

But as organizations mature, as they pivot from start-ups to successful incumbents, survival slips into the background. Reputation and brand protection become sacrosanct. Innovation and change become less necessities but more often threats. The organization and, perhaps the decisions made by previous leaders, are walled off from change both by bureaucracy and the hesitancy of an appointee who fears they might put at risk a hard-earned and now hallowed reputation. Editors might even be chosen for their “safe hands.”

Today, it is not our top-tier journals that are at risk, but our discipline. Even as digital technologies increasingly shape our economies, organizations, jobs, and lives, the field’s scholarship seems to grow ever more distant from its application and its societal implications. The _MISQ’s_ recent decision to require significance statements demonstrates that the journal realizes our research should be and can be more closely aligned with reality, a goal Gary Dickson strove for but failed to accomplish almost half a century ago.

I am pleased to see the _Quarterly_ continuing to play a leadership role in this and many other ways.

## Future Innovations We Need to Consider at MISQ

Reading the reflections from Blake Ives reminds us not only of how far we have come as a field but also of the consequences of our decisions and the challenges that lie ahead. What innovations must _MISQ_ consider in the future? Each new EIC and editorial team will have to consider this question. To provide food for thought, I asked two of my editorial board members to give us their views on how the journal may need to innovate in the coming years to address the opportunities and challenges of generative artificial intelligence. I asked Kai Larsen because of his ongoing research on digital innovations in scientific infrastructure, and Balaji Padmanabhan because he handles many of our papers on the forefront of artificial intelligence.

### Feeding, Observing, and Changing the Machine: The New Role of Researchers in the Era of Artificial General Intelligence? – Kai Larsen (Associate Editor, 2021-2024)

Humanity is at the doorstep of artificial general intelligence (AGI). In plural form, AGIs can be defined as “synthetic intelligences with sufficiently broad (e.g., human-level) scope and strong generalization capability” (Goertzel, 2014, p. 3). Such AGIs differ from special-purpose machine learning with lower generalization capabilities and from current generative AI, with the latter representing early, if incomplete, instances of AGI (Bubeck et al., 2023). In this section, I make the case that it is crucial for the IS discipline to get a jump-start on future AGIs and use this chance to better connect IS research to practice.

Future AGIs will likely offer a new class of intelligence, often beyond human intelligence. For a task worth paying a human to perform, we should expect AGIs and their present-day precursors to perform at least on par with humans trained in that class of tasks and take on a wide variety of such tasks. We can already get a sense of the likely capabilities of future AGIs from interacting with today’s generative AI.

Referring to Figure 1 (earlier), the current research enterprise invariably assumes that the world behind the text involves humans crafting knowledge for human consumption. This knowledge—in the form of text—has now become the fodder for AIs, which themselves are significant contributors to crafting knowledge (Susarla et al., 2023). In short, AIs are taking on more of the knowledge creation process—soon likely becoming the main contributor to the world of text.

Who are the intended consumers of research knowledge? Researchers are one set, but they cannot possibly find or consume all the knowledge in the millions of relevant papers (Larsen et al., 2019; Li et al., 2020). Practitioners are another (potential) set, but the problem is stark here. As Ives lamented above, the IS discipline lost its relevance for many practitioners. As a result, most practitioners carry on unaffected by the world of text (papers) and the world behind it (researchers). But that is not true for researchers; they depend on the world of text for employment and reputation. Their dependence also serves as a constraint. That is, if academics _really_ wanted to regain relevance in front of the text, I believe that they would need to truly transform the world behind it (even being willing to destroy current norms), something I fear they will not do.

While academics routinely talk about the implications of AI for knowledge creation (e.g., can I write this article with AI?), a larger disruption could come from the consumption side. Currently, papers are largely consumed by other researchers, not practitioners. Even when our research could be helpful to practitioners, Straub and Ang (2011) argue that it is rarely consumed directly in the form of articles, but is instead mediated by textbooks, classes, and public talks. However, such “translation” efforts are costly, poorly rewarded, and therefore function much like a wall. AGIs with chatbot interfaces represent a new type of knowledge interface that will undoubtedly overtake other sources (especially reading full articles or having to pay to hear from experts). This could—at least initially—be good news.

IS researchers should tackle this as a design problem. Consider the blueprint in Figure 2 for improving research relevance. The blueprint acknowledges the current wall between IS researchers/knowledge and practitioners, the general public, and researchers in other disciplines. However, it proposes that the class of future AGIs—and even the current class of chatbots—can breach the wall to reconnect knowledge production and consumption. The breach can be achieved by translating our complex research language to audience-understood language—as current large language models already do when prompted (Jeblick et al., 2022). As IS research is ingested and incorporated by AGIs, the knowledge should become available to all external stakeholders—in the interface they will use to get life and work advice. Any question for which IS papers provide knowledge should be answered with the benefit of such expertise, providing _relevance_.

Suppose we accept that AGIs—and even the current class of large language models—hold the key to communicating with practitioners and the public once more. If so, we must understand such “public AGIs.” This can come through behavioral research of what IS-relevant questions practitioners, the public, and researchers in other disciplines ask, or through design science efforts to build _disciplinary_ AGIs, or for now, chatbots and large language models. We could ask questions such as:

1. _How can we get machine designers to select IS journals?_ In all likelihood, designers will select articles that are easy and cheap to collect, such as articles in open repositories. Alternatively, simple metrics that apply across journals such as journal impact factors may be employed, with all the attendant risks to the discipline (Burton-Jones & Wang, 2023).

2. _How can we write up our research to be more digestible for the machine?_ IS literature is currently designed for human readers, e.g., with high-quality writing and perceptual novelty. If research is written for AGIs, papers may not need some sections. Shorter, more direct papers could be more useful, as some other sciences have already found.

3. _How can we design our research so that it changes the underlying knowledge structure of AGIs_? Suppose practitioners, first and foremost, interact with our crafted knowledge through AGIs. In that case, our research must first be ingested by AGIs before it can yield changes in the AGI’s knowledge structure. Should an article not cause an AGI to change a single answer to a question, perhaps that answer was already known, i.e., not novel. We must design disciplinary AGIs to better understand what constitutes AGI novelty and how we make review/publication decisions based on novelty.

4. _How should we design disciplinary AGIs?_ Research tasks have characteristics that are not as relevant to AGIs used by the public. Arguably, we need AGIs that are capable of reading, integrating, answering questions, and performing tasks on the IS academic literature and the literature of reference disciplines. We may also need access to the underlying structure of the AGIs to better explore questions such as knowledge novelty.

5. _How can we speed up our publication process?_ Do the last 2-4 rounds of peer review change a paper’s impact on an AGI enough to be worthwhile? How much more important is each round relative to writing another paper? In the AGI era, how will the IS discipline fare compared to other disciplines that accept papers within months rather than years?

6. _What are the IS-relevant questions asked of public AGIs?_ To develop relevant research that is novel to AGIs, we must know what questions practitioners ask public AGIs and which of these questions do not yield satisfactory answers. Such observation will allow us to feed the machine in a way that changes it for the better. While not illustrated in Figure 2, this suggests the potential for a thriving ecology of practice and research (Ramiller et al., 2008) where public and disciplinary AGIs serve as portals between IS researchers and practitioners in both directions.


Ives ended by stating that our discipline is at risk. Another way to look at it is that we stand at the doorstep of great change and opportunity. The sociotechnical skills necessary for profound innovation are among the IS discipline’s distinguishing characteristics, but we must be willing to break or even destroy some deeply embedded norms and practices.

### Opportunities of the Present – Balaji Padmanabhan (Associate Editor, 2016-2020; Senior Editor, 2021-2023)

Coming to age academically in the early 2000s, I never expected _MISQ_ to be a home for my research, so I see my existence on the editorial board as evidence of how the journal has innovated over the last 20 years to be more inclusive of all research genres. I am also fortunate to be serving on the board at a time of great innovation at the journal, because this has allowed me to give back to the journal a small fraction of what I have received over the years. For instance, I thoroughly enjoyed co-designing and running the masterclasses and knowledge-sharing sessions on machine learning research (Padmanabhan et al., 2022) and contributing to the transparency initiative (Burton-Jones et al., 2021). In this section, I would like to pivot beyond what has been said above and use Figures 1 and 2 to highlight some “opportunities of the present” for both the journal and our community.

During my time, I have seen our researchers progressively get closer to the “world in front of the text,” forging closer partnerships with industry than perhaps at any time in our field. _MISQ_ reviewers and editors have been very supportive of this trend while continuing to seek the highest-quality scholarship. This trend to get closer to the real world has also created a rush to study problems where real-world data is available. Somewhat akin to the streetlight effect, we have brilliant young minds looking for problems where there is data. Sometimes, unfortunately, this may not align with the grand challenges of our times. Pulling our resources together—including using the might of the journal to work with large enterprises or coalitions—to curate repositories of data (Goben & Sandusky, 2020), for instance in high-impact but currently less studied areas, might unleash new waves of innovation from our community in areas of great need.5 These efforts, by making available large and relevant repositories of data for problems of significance, could also help mitigate some of the tensions today between innovative work and transparency that review panels sometimes struggle with (which _MISQ’s_ transparency initiative has also highlighted).

The “world behind the text” might also get infinitely more exciting for two reasons at least: business model shifts in higher education and the coming of age of AI, both presenting significant opportunities we must attend to in the present. Regarding business model shifts, it is well-known that business schools have historically driven the tuition revenue of many universities. When the economy does well, other sources of funding (grants, public funds, endowment) tend to do well too. This “cushion” gives us (in business schools) flexibility in research that other schools lack. For years, we have been warned of black-swan events that could dramatically alter this equilibrium. If or when this happens, incentives could rapidly change for most of us to direct our research efforts to pressing needs in line with where the funding is. For _MISQ_ to support the field, it must consider editorial innovations to help rapidly identify and publish high-quality work that evolve with the needs of the times.

Regarding the age of AI, the world is preparing for significant change with excitement and fear. I endorse Larsen’s views on the impacts that generative AI and AGI will have on our research efforts as well as the thoughts in Susarla et al. (2023) and related commentaries. The possibilities of discipline-specific AGIs and their promise to interface with the “world in front of the text” are exciting. Here, I highlight four opportunities that I believe require additional attention:

1. _Rethinking source attribution_: Scholars contribute to science by building on prior contributions. The norms of science (behind the text) require that we define this contribution (in the text) with the relevant citations. A major weakness of traditional generative AI for science is its inability to precisely articulate where its generated knowledge was derived from. Generative AI creates new content from existing work without any correctness guarantees, which at the extreme can even create made-up (false) information called “hallucinations” (such as a citation that does not exist). Proper attribution can help verify the content that AI creates. As we build discipline-specific tools, we must make attribution a key requirement. In the interim, researchers using generative AI must indicate how their use of these tools ensures proper attribution of the underlying ideas—even if this is done as a post-processing or verification layer.

2. _Need for a verification layer:_ It is well-accepted that the content generated by AI tools needs verification (Tang et al., 2023). Indeed, the use of humans as part of a training strategy (i.e., reinforcement learning with human feedback; Ouyang et al., 2022) is known to have played a key role in the first generation of generative AI tools. As we build discipline-specific AGI tools, we must explicitly consider the design of a verification layer _specific to academic research_ and must evaluate how this might be scaled for the significantly more complex task of verifying scholarly statements.

3. _Need for transparency:_ Systems and tools built for the public good often end up helping some at the expense of others. Recommender systems, for instance, have been shown to have a popularity bias that might result in feedback loops that create unfair advantages for some (Prawesh & Padmanabhan, 2014). A discipline-specific AGI tool that would serve as our main interface with scholarly content would have tremendous power but could prove biased (failing to uphold a “responsible science”) unless we design for it. One solution would be full transparency (code, data, decisions made and by whom) in the development of such tools that could help the community assess and mitigate some of these risks. Yet, even intentional design does not eliminate bias, but rather aligns it to whatever values are implicit in such design. Gabriel (2020) presents some thoughtful considerations on AI alignment that we might need to reflect on as we consider these possibilities.

4. _Designing for variants:_ Discipline-specific AI tools do not have to be “one size fits all” and might be engineered to have variants based on who interacts with them and their goals for doing so. Some variants might be “cautious” (built on only very well-documented and highly replicated work of established scholars), “young” (emphasizing new knowledge/scholars more than old), or “bold” (taking more chances when “connecting the dots” across ideas). Having different lenses to interact with our own knowledge bases—each designed with full transparency—can create new ways of creating both incremental and pathbreaking ideas that we might not even be able to conceive of today. While each such variant will be aligned (Gabriel 2020) with specific values and goals, envisioning a future where researchers might individually direct this alignment as the level of maturity (of both the tools and our use) increases is exciting because of the power and flexibility it can provide to future researchers looking to add to a large and increasing complex knowledge base about our world.


_MISQ_’s role in enabling such innovations inspired by “opportunities of the present” needs more reflection. The editorial board and publisher must be willing to innovate, but we must also encourage and act on innovations that rise bottom-up from the field. Developing policies and/or workshops for the community around these ideas could help build momentum and progress. Building formal and informal bridges across leading journals in business and other disciplines will help us learn from and influence best practices in other fields. The best innovations may simply arise when we do nothing that gets in the way of the great minds in our field and let them lead the way, as they have done before. All options must be on the table.

## Conclusion

I hope you have finished this editorial with a clear idea that innovation is part of _MISQ_’s DNA. In my opening editorial (Burton-Jones, 2021), I wrote that _MISQ_ board members are “institutional custodians” in that we believe deeply in the institutions of our field and support them at every occasion. While this is true, it does not suggest that we are a conservative journal unable to take risks, because innovation is itself one of the institutions of the field. However, our abilities to innovate are not boundless. After reading the editorial, I hope you have a clearer idea of some of the constraints we face. As Blake Ives noted, _MISQ_ editors have been trying to balance these opportunities and constraints as well as possible throughout the journal’s history.

How should we innovate for the challenges and opportunities that lie ahead? I asked two of my current editors (Kai Larsen and Balaji Padmanabhan) for their views rather than offering my own because we work collectively at _MISQ_. We currently have 73 editors, and we all work as hard as we can to offer ideas and make decisions. Differences in opinion arise frequently and are respected. Each year, as new editors come into the journal, new ideas will be offered, and new practices instituted. As Padmanabhan notes above, we do not expect to know all the answers; we look to you, the reader, for your innovations too.

One of _MISQ_’s greatest strengths is that we support every genre of our field. Scholars from different research genres will naturally look at the challenges and opportunities we face differently. Scholars aligned with explanatory traditions will search for novel ways to explain and predict what is happening in the world. Design scientists will try to build new engines to power progress to a better future. Interpretivists will seek new ways to appreciate human meaning in an increasingly complex and confusing world. Critical theorists will urge us to reveal the inequities that surround us and provoke action. We need innovation from all the genres in our field to navigate the roads ahead. We look forward to innovating with you.
